The most awkward part of any Indian friendship is money. We built an app to remove it.

India pays in ₹, verifies by phone, settles on UPI, and increasingly talks to its software. So we built an expense-splitter that assumes all four — free, and AI-first where it earns its place.

From the Dutcho team

The ₹200 you never ask for

Every Indian friend group has a quiet ledger nobody wants to open. Someone covered the Uber. Someone paid for dinner because their UPI worked and yours didn't. Someone booked the whole Goa stay on their card "and we'll settle later." Later never quite comes.

We don't chase it because ₹200 isn't worth a weird conversation. So it compounds — across trips, rent, tiffin, the Sunday lunch crew — until the person who always pays quietly stops suggesting plans. The money was never the problem. The asking was.

We built Dutcho because we were tired of being both people in that story: the one who pays, and the one who forgets.

Built for how India actually splits

Most expense apps were built for a different wallet — dollars, manual entry, the features you actually want tucked behind a paywall, and a shrug toward "settle outside the app" when it's finally time to pay. In India, paying back happens on UPI. So the app should just take you there.

The gap was never a missing feature. It was a point of view. India pays in ₹, verifies by phone, settles on UPI, and increasingly talks to its software. An expense-splitter built here should assume all four.

What "AI-first" actually means when it's about money

"AI" is the most oversold word in tech right now, so let us be precise about where it earns its place in a money app — and where we deliberately keep it out.

  • You describe the expense; you don't fill a form. Type "1200 dinner with Sam, I paid" and Dutcho works out who's in, who paid, and who owes what. No dropdowns.
  • You scan the receipt; it splits by item. The chai you didn't drink isn't your share. Point your camera at the bill and assign items — the maths for tax and tip spreads itself, to the paisa.
  • You ask Dutcho your own money questions. "How much did I spend on food this month?" gets a straight answer, computed from your real expenses.

And the line we will not cross: the numbers never come from the AI. Language models are good at understanding "1200 dinner with Sam." They are not allowed anywhere near the arithmetic. Every balance is computed deterministically on our server and always adds up to zero. On a money app, trust is the product — cleverness that can't be verified is a liability, not a feature. So the AI reads your intent; the ledger does the math.

The part everyone forgets: actually paying back

Tracking a debt is half a solution. Dutcho closes the loop where India already lives — UPI. When you owe someone, you settle straight through GPay, PhonePe, Paytm or BHIM, and the balance clears. No "I'll send it later." No screenshots as proof. Just done.

Accounts are phone-verified, so the "Rahul" in your group is the actual Rahul — no impersonation, no confusion about who owes whom. It works offline too, because Indian networks don't care that you're mid-split in a basement café.

Built in the open, with AI as a teammate

Dutcho is being built largely with Claude Code as a development partner — which feels right for an app whose whole thesis is that good AI removes drudgery so humans can do the human part. We're early and honest about it: Android 1.0.17 is live now at dutcho.app, iOS is on the App Store, and the Play Store listing is in closed testing.

To thank the people who show up early, the first 150 users become Founding Members — a full year of Dutcho Pro and a PRO badge on their profile, free. Not a growth gimmick; a genuine thank-you to the folks willing to try version-early software and tell us what's broken.

The real goal

Dutcho isn't trying to make you think about money more. It's trying to make you think about it less — so the ₹200 never becomes a reason a friendship gets quieter. Split the bill, settle on UPI, move on to the next trip.
