Equal, exact, or by item: which way to split a bill?

There isn't one right way to divide a bill — there are three, and the fair choice depends on the situation. Here's when to use each, and how to handle the edge cases that start arguments.

Guide · about 5 min read

Short version

Split equally when everyone shared roughly the same. Split by exact amounts when you already know each person's number. Split by item when what people ordered varied a lot. Spread tax and tip in proportion to what each person ordered, and don't sweat sub-₹20 differences.

Most "how should we split this?" standoffs happen because two people are silently assuming different methods. One thinks "obviously we go halves"; the other is mentally adding up exactly what they ate. Naming the three methods — and knowing when each is fair — ends the standoff before it starts.

1. Equal split

When it's fair: everyone consumed roughly the same, or the differences are small enough that nobody genuinely minds. A group dinner where everyone had a main and shared a couple of plates. A cab everyone rode. The Wi-Fi bill.

Equal splitting has one big virtue beyond simplicity: it's generous by default. It quietly says "we're not counting". Among close friends, that spirit is often worth more than a few rupees of precision — insisting on itemising a casual dinner among friends can feel colder than the money saved.

The trap: equal splitting stops being fair the moment one person had a ₹1,200 steak and another had a ₹150 soup. That's when you switch methods.

2. Exact amounts

When it's fair: you already know exactly what each person owes — because they told you, or because it's a fixed cost per person. Concert tickets at ₹2,500 each. A group gift where people chipped in different amounts. A bill someone already worked out.

This is less a "method" than a way to record numbers you already have. The point is to enter each person's real figure rather than forcing it into an even split. Good splitting tools let you type each person's exact amount and just check that the parts add up to the total.

3. Item by item

When it's fair: what people ordered varied a lot, and an equal split would genuinely overcharge the light eaters. A restaurant bill where some drank and some didn't; a grocery run that mixed shared staples with one person's personal shopping.

Here you assign each line item to the people who shared it — this dish was Priya and Sam's, that one was just mine, the shared starters go to everyone — and each person pays for what they actually had.

How to handle tax and tip on an itemised bill

Don't split tax and tip equally on top of an itemised bill — that quietly overcharges the person who ordered less. Instead, spread them in proportion to what each person's items came to. If your food was 30% of the pre-tax total, you cover 30% of the tax and tip too. Most good splitting apps do this automatically so the parts still sum to the exact grand total.

The person who "only had a salad"

Every group has the moment where one person had far less than everyone else and doesn't want to pay an equal share. They're not being difficult — an equal split really would overcharge them. The fix is simply to switch methods: itemise, or carve their smaller amount out as an exact figure, and split the rest equally among the others. Naming the method out loud — "let's just do this one by item" — turns a potential sulk into a non-event.

A simple decision rule

  1. Did everyone share roughly equally? → Equal.
  2. Do you already know each person's number? → Exact.
  3. Did what people ordered vary a lot? → By item.

And whichever you pick, remember the meta-rule: the goal is for everyone to feel the split was fair, not for the arithmetic to be perfect to the paisa. Chasing tiny differences costs more goodwill than it saves.

Fair isn't one formula. It's picking the method that matches how the money was actually spent — and saying so out loud.
